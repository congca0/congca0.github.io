## From Environment to Precision Health

Growing up in a region of China with a large coal-mining industry, I became interested early on in environmental health and the relationship between environmental exposures and human health. I originally trained in pharmaceutical engineering and later worked in the medical technology industry, where I collaborated with physicians and software engineers on healthcare information systems. These experiences introduced me to both the complexity of healthcare and the role that quantitative methods can play in understanding and improving it.

I then moved into biomedical and computational research in Germany. At the Helmholtz Centre for Infection Research, I used machine learning and mass spectrometry data to study herpesvirus. At the University of Göttingen, I worked with mRNA sequencing and single-cell transcriptomic data. Working across these biological questions and data types gave me an early foundation in biomedical data science and taught me to approach scientific problems from both biological and computational perspectives.

During my Ph.D. in Economics, with a focus on machine learning, at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, I returned to environmental health from a different perspective. Using nationwide data from Norway, I studied relationships between air pollution, weather, and cardiovascular health and developed statistical and machine learning methods to characterize nonlinear and delayed effects. My training in economics also gave me a strong foundation in econometrics and causal inference, which became an important part of my research toolkit.

At Caltech, I broadened my work through collaborations with clinicians, epidemiologists, and biomedical scientists. My research included environmental exposures, aging, epigenetics, and postoperative outcomes. I also served as a Lead Investigator on an NIH-funded multicenter project examining climate-related exposures, air pollution, and aging using clinical and molecular data. These experiences moved my work further toward population health and large-scale biomedical research, while exposing me to a wider range of clinical and translational questions.

At Yale, I moved more directly into causal AI for health research. Through collaborations with investigators in the VA Cooperative Studies Program, I studied treatment effects using large-scale observational and clinical trial data. This work brought together my earlier training in machine learning and econometrics with real-world health research and strengthened my interest in causal methods for understanding variation in treatment outcomes.

I am now a Postdoctoral Fellow at Stanford University, where I work on AI for precision medicine using biomedical data. My current research builds on this broader trajectory, combining ideas from causal inference, machine learning, and population health to develop quantitative approaches for understanding health and treatment outcomes.

A common thread runs through these experiences: I am interested in understanding why health outcomes vary across people, environments, and treatments, and in developing quantitative tools that can help explain and address that variation. My path has taken me across environmental health, biomedical science, economics, AI, and causal inference, but the underlying goal has remained consistent—to use quantitative methods to better understand human health and ultimately contribute to better population health and precision medicine.
